How Mello works

Every video gets five measurements, each mapped onto a calm → intense scale, then combined into one score from 0 to 100. The score is relative to the kids' shows we scored, not an absolute medical threshold.

What we sample

We don't score whole videos. For each one we take three one-minute windows at 20%, 50% and 80% of its length and analyse them together. The opening is skipped on purpose: intros and theme songs are cut faster than the body of a show. Videos under four minutes are taken whole. Each entry is the top YouTube search result for the show's name, downloaded at 360p; the title and link are shown on its card so you can check what was measured.

The five metrics

MetricHow it's measuredCalm end → intense end
Cuts per minuteHard scene changes, found with PySceneDetect's content detector (it compares colour and edges between consecutive frames and flags a cut when the change spikes). Divided by the sampled length.5 → 30 per minute
On-screen motionHow much the picture changes between frames even when there is no cut. We take 4 frames a second in greyscale at 160 px wide, average the pixel difference between consecutive frames, drop the frames that are cuts, and take the median. Shown ×1000.20 → 120
LoudnessAverage level of the soundtrack in decibels (dBFS) over 100 ms windows, ignoring silent windows. 0 dB is the loudest a file can be; −40 dB is very quiet.−40 → −12 dB
Non-stop soundShare of 100 ms windows above −40 dBFS, i.e. how much of the time there is any sound at all. A show that pauses between lines scores lower here.60% → 100%
Colour saturationOne frame a second, converted to HSV; the mean saturation channel. 0% is greyscale, 100% is fully vivid colour.35% → 65%

From metrics to a score

Each measurement is placed on its scale: the calm end counts as 0, the intense end as 100, anything beyond the ends is clipped. Those five sub-scores are weighted and added:

cuts 30% · motion 30% · loudness 20% · non-stop sound 10% · saturation 10%

Cuts and motion carry the most weight because they separated the shows most clearly in testing. The result becomes a label: Low under 40, Medium 40–64, High 65 and up.

How we set the scales

The scale ends were chosen by scoring a reference set of 41 shows parents named in our survey and interviews, from Bluey and Oswald at the calm end to Cocomelon and Vlad and Niki at the intense end. We then checked the ranking three ways: scoring different episodes of the same show (three Cocomelon videos landed 66–77, all High), re-running with all five metrics weighted equally, and re-running with motion removed entirely. The order of shows barely moved in any of the three.

What the research says about pacing

The pediatric literature uses the same variable. Lillard & Peterson (Pediatrics, 2011) found 4-year-olds did worse on self-control tasks right after nine minutes of a cartoon that cut every 11 seconds, versus one that cut every 34. A 2024 study of 864 toddlers (Frontiers in Public Health) defined fast-paced as a cut every 10 seconds or less and found higher difficulty scores in children who mostly watched it. The American Academy of Pediatrics advises against fast-paced programmes for under-fives and has said Cocomelon's pace draws expert criticism, while noting no study has tested Cocomelon itself. By the research definition (a cut every ≤10 s, about 6 per minute), nearly every show here is fast-paced; our bands compare shows to each other, not to that older baseline.

Known limits

  • One video per show, mostly. A show's score is the average of the videos we scored for it. Different episodes vary; Cocomelon's three videos span 66–77.
  • Live action filmed handheld (Blippi) has few hard cuts; its intensity shows up as motion and loudness instead, so it reads lower than its reputation.
  • Old cartoons (Tom and Jerry) have quiet mono audio and faded colour, which pulls them down regardless of pacing.
  • Trailers and compilations are cut faster than episodes. We try to score episodes; the link on each card shows what was used.
  • Not measured yet: on-screen text overlays, word repetition, and musical hooks, all of which parents mentioned.
  • Not a health claim. The score describes how a video is made. It says nothing about whether a particular child should watch it.
